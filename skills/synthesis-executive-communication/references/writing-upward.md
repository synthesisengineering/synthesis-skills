# Writing upward: answer first, never correct, sell rather than argue

Writing to someone with more power than you (a chief executive, a board member, a client's executive, your
own manager) is a register of its own. A case that would persuade a peer can still fail with this reader,
because a powerful reader accepts a case that leaves their authority, their choices and their self-image
intact, and resents one that corrects them, however accurate it is.

## The rule

Give the reader what they asked for first, in their terms and as a success. Make the rest of the case the
way a seller makes it, not the way a debater does. Leave their authority, their choices and their
self-image intact.

## The edits that make a sound case acceptable

The examples illustrate a common case, invented for this page: an executive from outside software asks
when a software product will be "done," shortly before a meeting about it. Every edit generalizes to any
powerful reader.

1. **Answer the question first, with a win.** The reader came for "done," so the first line says what is
   done and frames it as an achievement that credits the team. Everything after it then reads as what comes
   next, not as an objection.
2. **Remove every line that implies they were wrong.** "Products don't finish" may be accurate and still
   tells the reader the question was mistaken. The positive form ("products keep shipping new versions")
   makes the same point by describing what good products do. Watch for "X, not Y," "doesn't," "never" and
   "nothing" constructions, and for anything that reads as a gotcha.
3. **Offer a distinction they can adopt.** "A product vs a project" invites the reader to take the
   distinction as their own idea; "a product, not a project" corrects them.
4. **Never tell them how to run their time or meeting.** "So we can spend the time on decisions" instructs
   the organizer; "to help with decisions" offers support.
5. **Pick examples from the reader's world, not the writer's.** One line about products the reader knows
   from the news does more than any principle about software.
6. **Fit analogies to where the reader is taking the organization.** An analogy from the business the
   organization is leaving pulls a reader who is reinventing it back toward the identity they are shedding.
   Use the future they are building.
7. **Argue as a seller.** Make the case from a buyer's point of view, with the alternatives in view: why
   customers will pay, and keep paying, compared with their other options. That is how the reader's board
   and customers think.
8. **Put the upside on the page.** Tie the most ambitious option to the reader's own ambition, so a staged
   recommendation reads as the path to the biggest upside, not as caution.
9. **Calibrate cost honestly, in reassuring words.** "A small standing team, sized to the level we choose"
   reassures and stays true. Never let reassurance slide into "negligible," "free" or "maintenance mode";
   that sets an expectation the work can't meet.
10. **Don't settle choices the reader has reserved.** If they have kept a question for themselves or
    scheduled it for another meeting, word the note so it stays open.
11. **"We" for accountability; "you" only for their decisions.** "We'll track it" shares the accountability.
    "You'll track it" casts the reader as an auditor checking on the writer.
12. **Treat the hierarchy evenly.** Brief your own manager first and ask for their support. Address every
    reader of the note equally, by name, and never rank the most senior one above your manager.
13. **Make it read as a person's email.** No document title or byline; a subject tied to the meeting it
    serves; thanks and your normal signature. Senior readers discount anything that looks machine-assembled.

## What drafts get wrong

- **A milestone that contradicts reality.** Check the product's real status before framing a milestone; a
  draft that proposes declaring "1.0" ahead, for something already in daily use, undermines everything else
  in the note.
- **The senior reader first, your manager on copy.** Fix the addressing before anything else.
- **The "of course" analogy from the reader's past.**
- **Edges a peer would accept and a powerful reader resents:** a correction in the subject line, a gotcha,
  instructions about their meeting.

## Pre-send check

- **First line:** does it give the reader what they asked for, as a success, in their words?
- **Correction scan:** remove any sentence that implies their premise was wrong; rewrite negative
  constructions in the positive form.
- **Agency:** options with a recommendation and a decision for them to make; nothing about how to run their
  time or meeting.
- **Reader model:** where are they taking the organization? Examples and analogies from that future and from
  what they know firsthand.
- **Their concerns behind the question:** time, cost, revenue, risk, a story for their own board. Answer those,
  not only the literal question.
- **Honest calibration:** nothing implied free, finished or self-running that isn't.
- **Reserved choices:** left open.
- **Pronouns:** "we" for accountability and plans; "you" only for their decisions.
- **Hierarchy:** your manager briefed first, with an ask for support; every reader addressed equally.
- **Form:** a person's email, readable on a phone in two minutes, its subject tied to the meeting.
- **Timing:** when the reader will set the tone in the first minute of a meeting, send the note before it,
  and ask an ally to open from it.
- **Last pass:** read it once for typos. Time pressure produces them, and a typo is the flaw a senior reader
  always sees.
